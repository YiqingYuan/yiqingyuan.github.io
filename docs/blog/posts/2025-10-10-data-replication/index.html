Global Innovation and Technology Transfer in Low-Carbon Transition

Data Analysis using PATSTAT Database

research
This post summarizes and visualizes key patterns in low-carbon energy innovation and international technology transfer in past decades, mainly based on PATSTAT data.
Published

October 10, 2025

Overview

This post presents a research summary based on my data replication project using PATSTAT (Autumn 2024 edition), focusing on low-carbon energy (LCE) technologies and their international transfer patterns.

The full report is available here:

👉 Download the full replication report (PDF)

Below is an AI-generated overview of my report.

What Is an Innovation and a Transfer?

  • Innovation is defined at the level of a simple patent family, following Dechezleprêtre et al. (2011) and subsequent literature.
  • Inventor country is proxied by the priority patent authority.
  • Technology transfer occurs when a patent family is filed in at least one authority different from its priority authority, forming an international patent family (IPF).

These definitions allow consistent global comparisons over a long time horizon (1900–2022).

Data Coverage

  • Source: PATSTAT (Autumn 2024, EPO)
  • Scope:
    • ~4.9 million low-carbon energy inventions
    • 96 patent authorities
  • Time window:
    • Innovation trends: 1990–2020
    • Transfer dynamics: 1900–2022

A major challenge is missing inventor-location information. I adopt a baseline method from the literature and implement an adjusted imputation strategy using subsequent filings.

Global Trends in Low-Carbon Innovation

Innovation by Inventor Country

The first pattern is the rapid take-off of East Asian innovation, especially China, since the early 2000s.

Key facts: - China overtakes all other countries in cumulative LCE inventions after 2010. - Japan and Korea also show sustained growth. - Most developing countries remain far behind in absolute innovation counts.

(Corresponds to Figure 1 in the PDF report.)

Innovation by Technology Field

LCE inventions are grouped into three major fields: - Energy supply - End-use technologies - Enabling technologies

Across all three fields: - China’s growth is broad-based rather than field-specific. - Enabling technologies (e.g. batteries, smart grids) expand particularly fast after 2005.

How Often Do Technologies Transfer Internationally?

Aggregate Transfer Rates

Despite strong growth in innovation, transfer rates are far from universal.

Main observations: - Only a fraction of inventions become international patent families. - Transfer rates peak in the mid–20th century and decline afterward. - Enabling technologies show consistently higher transfer rates than energy supply.

(See Figure 4 in the PDF.)

Transfer by Technology Subclass

At a more granular level: - Batteries, hydrogen, and CCUS exhibit relatively high transfer rates. - Many end-use technologies remain largely domestic.

This suggests that technology characteristics, not just country size, shape diffusion.

Who Transfers to Whom?

Transfers Between Income Groups

A striking asymmetry emerges: - Most transfers occur within high-income countries. - Transfers from high-income to middle-income countries increase over time. - Transfers involving low-income countries remain extremely rare.

Even in recent decades, South–South and North–South transfers are limited.

China’s Role in Global Technology Transfer

China plays a growing role in global transfers, but with an important caveat:

  • China’s outbound transfers rise mainly because China innovates more.
  • The structure of destinations (high vs. middle income) remains relatively stable.
  • China does not disproportionately redirect transfers toward low-income countries.

This suggests scale effects dominate structural change.

Why This Matters

These findings highlight a core tension in climate innovation: - Innovation is increasingly global. - Diffusion remains highly uneven.

Understanding why technologies fail to transfer—despite clear global benefits— is central to designing effective climate and industrial policies.

What Comes Next

This replication is the foundation for ongoing work on: - Barriers to international technology diffusion - The role of institutions and market size - China’s position in global innovation networks

Future extensions will integrate original patent-office data and explore policy-induced changes in transfer patterns.
